My research combines quantitative experiments with models developed in close dialogue with theorists, to understand how phase change, interfacial flows and the properties of the surrounding medium couple in confined multiphase systems.

<div class="project" markdown="1">
## Nucleation & condensation in polymer mixtures
<p class="lead">Biomimetic models of cellular condensates.</p>

Classical nucleation theory is notoriously hard to test, because critical nuclei form at the nanometer scale, far below optical resolution. I study condensation in polymer solutions that demix under temperature, where the low mixing energy pushes critical radii up to the micrometer scale — large enough to be imaged directly. Using thermostated microfluidic chips and confocal microscopy, we follow nucleation, growth and coarsening in real time, and probe the pre-nucleation state by small-angle X-ray scattering at synchrotron SOLEIL. Beyond the experimental advantage, these phase-separating mixtures are minimal models of the membraneless biomolecular condensates found in living cells, whose arrested growth still lacks a clear explanation. This project is funded by an ANR JCJC grant (ANR-25-CE30-2819) and carried out with Mathilde Reyssat and Élie Raphaël (Gulliver, ESPCI), with Alice Mougey's PhD at its core.

</div>

## Thermocapillary (de)stabilization of surface bubbles
<p class="lead">When a small temperature difference decides whether a film lives or dies.</p>

A bare liquid film should drain and burst within milliseconds, yet surface bubbles can survive for seconds when a temperature gradient runs across them. We showed that the evaporative cooling of a bubble's apex sets up upward Marangoni flows that oppose gravitational drainage and select the film thickness — a thermal alternative to stabilization by surfactants, demonstrated both for Leidenfrost chimneys and for bubbles on heated silicone oil. The reverse is also true: heating the top of a viscous bubble by just one degree drives downward Marangoni flows that accelerate thinning and trigger early rupture. This dual control — stabilizing or destabilizing at will — has drawn the interest of Saint-Gobain, for whom bubbles at the surface of molten glass baths spoil the final material.

<p class="refs">Related: <a href="/publications/">Thermal Marangoni bubbles</a> (Soft Matter 2022). Work with Wilfried Raff (intern).</p>
</div>

## How surface bubbles accelerate evaporation
<p class="lead">A bubble turns a slow diffusive process into a convective jet.</p>

Evaporation from a confined liquid usually follows a slow diffusive law, with the interface receding as the square root of time. We found that placing a single air bubble at the surface of a volatile liquid can speed this up by up to an order of magnitude. Schlieren imaging reveals why: thermocapillary flows along the bubble — driven by the evaporative cooling of its cap — funnel the surrounding vapor and eject it as a centimeter-scale convective plume, short-circuiting the diffusive bottleneck. A scaling model linking plume flux to bubble size matches independent mass-loss measurements. The result speaks to natural processes such as sea-spray aerosol production and drying in porous media, and naturally raises the question of water — the focus of Clémence Fléchelle's upcoming thesis.

<p class="refs">From Xue Ma's PhD, with Axel Huerre and Matthieu Roché (MSC). Submitted, 2026.</p>
</div>

<div class="project" markdown="1">
## Non-wetting by heat and motion
<p class="lead">Two ways to repel a liquid, and what happens when you combine them.</p>

A drop can be kept from wetting a solid either by heating the surface past the Leidenfrost point or by moving it fast enough — both create a thin gas layer that prevents contact. We ask whether these strategies add up. Millimetric drops are launched onto a horizontal substrate held at temperature *T* with a tangential velocity *v*, and we measure the threshold temperature for levitation. It drops sharply with speed: from about 200 °C at rest down to room temperature near 1.35 m/s, where drops levitate "cold." This hybrid regime is exactly what glass-wool manufacturing faces, where binder droplets meet hot moving fibers. The work, framed by lubrication and scaling laws, builds a unified picture of non-wetting based on gas lubrication.

<p class="refs">Tristan Simon's CIFRE PhD with Saint-Gobain, co-supervised with Martin Coux and Laurence Talini (SVI). Related: <a href="/publications/">Aerodynamic repellency of impacting liquids</a> (PRF 2018).</p>
</div>

## Drying of salted hydrogels: model soils for salt deserts
<p class="lead">Reproducing the polygons of a salt flat in a transparent gel.</p>

The striking polygonal crusts of salt deserts like Uyuni are thought to arise from evaporation coupled to solutal convection in the porous ground. To test this, we dry salted agar hydrogels — transparent, controllable analogues of porous soils — and watch the flows driven by salt accumulation, the onset of instability, and the spatial scales it selects. The goal is to connect evaporation, solute transport and crystallization to the emergence of organized patterns. The project is funded by an IdEx Émergence grant, which equips a setup with controlled temperature, humidity and ventilation, and supports a PhD student arriving in 2026.

<p class="refs">Part of my broader work on evaporation and thermo-convective instabilities in confined media.</p>
